Katherine Trieu · Grade 12 · Ho Chi Minh City

Hi, I’m Gia Hân.

I have always been interested in the small choices that change how people act around one another. I first began noticing them while sitting on the floor of an art workshop. Since then, I have kept following the question into shelters, research sessions, digital products and community events, trying to understand why some spaces make it easier for people to take part.

01Notice

A tray in the middle

When I first started designing art sessions for Nét Mơ, I paid most attention to the activity itself. I thought that if the prompt was creative enough, children would naturally want to join.

Then, during one session, I noticed that every child had their own box of crayons. They drew carefully, but mostly alone. The next time, we placed one tray in the middle. Soon, hands were crossing over the table. Children began asking each other for colours, leaning over neighbouring pages and deciding together which green belonged to the grass.

The activity had barely changed, but the room had. After that, I began paying attention to choices I had previously treated as background. We used story worlds when “draw whatever you want” felt too open. We changed the arrangement of the tables and rewrote questions when they asked children to explain too much, too soon.

Working on Nét Mơ made me more excited about what art could do, but also more careful about what I claimed it could do. After many Saturdays, I had notebooks full of observations. I wanted to know whether those patterns existed beyond the sessions I remembered most clearly.

02Study

Testing what I thought I knew

To find out, I designed a study with 68 young adults. Each participant completed two sessions. In one, they worked through an experience with art materials. In the other, they reflected through a dialogue with AI.

I expected one method to feel more natural or personal. Instead, participants responded differently depending partly on how familiar they already were with art. For some, making something with their hands gave them an easier place to begin. For others, conversation did.

I stopped asking which method was better and became more interested in fit. What does this person already feel comfortable doing? Where do they hesitate? What kind of first step asks enough of them without asking too much?

03Make

Keeping the days we missed

A related problem was appearing inside the project teams I worked with. Our members lived in different cities and countries, so someone was almost always absent. They could read the official update and understand what we had completed, but that was not the same as knowing how the day had felt.

They missed the photograph taken while packing supplies, the voice note recorded on the way home and the small stories that disappeared inside a busy group chat.

I co-founded The Colorful Journey with a five-person team to give those moments somewhere to stay. I designed the product structure and UX/UI around a shared timeline where members could leave writing, photographs and voice notes, move between projects and dates, and rediscover old entries through “On This Day.”

More than 550 people visited, and over 450 returned. That second number mattered because we had made somewhere people wanted to revisit.

04Gather

Making room at a larger scale

The same attention shaped how I approached larger events. At Beats of Hope, more than ten student bands performed for an audience of over 300, helping us raise 25 million VND for hospital programming. At school, I also worked on a football league, a water-rocket competition and a science festival.

Much of that work involved budgets, permission forms and schedules. What kept drawing me back was the moment an event stopped feeling like something we had organised and began to belong to the people inside it.

It happened when the first student stepped onto the pitch, when a team ran after a rocket that had finally flown straight, and when one person moved closer to the stage and others followed. Those moments taught me that participation begins before an event starts. It begins with how someone is invited and whether taking the first step feels possible.

05Draw

Where I began

Long before I knew how to study any of these questions, I drew. I have been drawing since I was five, and it remains the way I stay with something when my first explanation feels incomplete.

My paintings often return to perception, memory and emotion, not because they illustrate my research, but because many of them helped me notice the questions before I knew how to name them.

06The question

The question I am still following

When I look across a shelter dining hall, a research session, a shared timeline and a concert floor, I do not see unrelated activities. I see different versions of the same thing I am still trying to understand.

Why does one person lean in while another stays near the edge? What makes an experience begin to feel like theirs too? How much can change when we move one tray, rewrite one prompt or give a memory somewhere to return?
